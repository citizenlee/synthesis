Background
Benchtop nucleic acid synthesis devices provide rapid, decentralized access to synthetic DNA and
are used by life sciences research labs, academic core facilities, pharmaceutical companies,
diagnostic laboratories, and small-scale mail-order synthesis companies1. Given the dual-use
potential of synthetic DNA, recent policy proposals have called for extending sequence and
customer screening obligations from centralized commercial synthesis providers to manufacturers
of benchtop synthesis devices. However, the challenges of adapting screening to benchtop devices
have yet to be adequately addressed2.
These preliminary findings summarize our survey of the benchtop nucleic acid synthesis
landscape, examining all benchtop devices that are currently available, under development, or
discontinued. Our forthcoming report will provide more detail on benchtop device types and
capabilities, map the global distribution of manufacturers, assess regulatory and policy definitions
of benchtop devices, and compare technical safeguards. We expect this comprehensive
assessment of the benchtop nucleic acid synthesis landscape will provide valuable insights to
guide the development of effective policy frameworks and technical safeguards, supporting the
responsible adoption of these technologies while addressing emerging biosecurity risks.

Current landscape of benchtop synthesis devices
Commercially available nucleic acid synthesis devices come in a range of sizes, from small to large
benchtop instruments and floor-standing systems (Fig. 1A). While most benchtop devices employ
either chemical phosphoramidite-based synthesis or enzymatic synthesis, a variety of other
formats exist (Fig. 1B). Phosphoramidite-based systems include conventional column- or
well-based formats as well as array-based approaches, in which synthesis sites may be addressed
using electrochemical control, droplet deposition, photolithographic deprotection, or other
localized reactions. Enzymatic synthesis represents an alternative approach based on nucleotide
incorporation by enzymes such as engineered terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase3-6. Analysis of
available device specifications and synthesis capacities shows that array-based devices have the
highest synthesis throughput capabilities, evidenced by their ability to synthesize as many as 1.5
billion unique nucleotides across up to 4,350,000 sites per run (Fig. 1C, D).

Among the benchtop synthesis devices identified in this survey, device manufacturers were
headquartered across eleven countries: Austria, Canada, China, Denmark, France, Germany,
Japan, Russia, Sweden, the United Kingdom, and the United States (USA). Among these, China
(31.8%), the USA (25%), and Japan (11.8%) contribute the highest numbers of benchtop device
models, while the total share across the EU is 16.8% (Fig. 2A).
The first benchtop synthesis devices released on the market in the 1980s relied predominantly on
conventional phosphoramidite chemistry for DNA synthesis. In the decade from 2006 to 2015, an
average of 3.7 new benchtop device models emerged annually. The release rate has since
increased significantly, with an average of 9.3 new models per year in the following decade from
2016 to 2025 (Fig. 2B). Though the exact release date was not publicly available for every model,
our comprehensive analysis of publicly available data found that an estimated total of 280 device
models have been introduced since the early 1980s, 102 of which have since been discontinued
from production.

Safeguarding benchtop synthesis devices
Currently, there are no international regulatory requirements or consensus best practices for
benchtop device biosecurity2. The November 2026 US National Policy for Nucleic Acid Synthesis
Screening establishes obligations for device manufacturers and calls for the creation of technical
standards for synthesis screening on benchtop devices, including the form, issuance,
cryptographic verification, physical security, and validity of authorization issued by verified
screening tools to individual benchtop devices7.
Safeguards for benchtop devices can be implemented through a layered approach encompassing
know-your-customer (KYC) screening, operator-device interactions, software and hardware
controls, and record-keeping mechanisms (Fig. 3).

   ●​ KYC screening: In addition to KYC checks at the point of device purchase or transfer,
      proprietary consumables (reagents, kits, cartridges) create additional opportunities for
      ongoing customer or site verification, including for devices sold to third parties.
   ●​ Operator-device interactions: Device use can be secured by ensuring users are uniquely
      authenticated through tiered licensing and multi-factor authentication, pairing licenses for
      individual access levels with cryptographic credentials or electronic signatures. KYC may
      be used to set a default tier on each device.
   ●​ Software controls: Synthesis requests can then be authorized using networked
      authorization or “phone-home” screening (requiring careful secure handling of proprietary
      sequences)8 or offline screening (requiring careful secure handling of screening databases
      and software updates).
   ●​ Hardware controls: Software-only screening and authorization can be bypassed unless
      devices implement hardware safeguards. For example, secure boot backed by a hardware
      root of trust can ensure that firmware and low-level controllers for electronics, fluidics, and
      thermal modules execute only signed jobs, preventing untrusted actuation of synthesis
      hardware. Additionally, tamper-proofing mechanisms could be used to detect
      unauthorized physical access to device components, triggering lockout or requiring
      inspection to resume operation.
   ●​ Record-keeping: At either a hardware or software level, devices can implement an audit
      log to enable attribution of sequences synthesized on a device9 and automated reporting
      to ensure that attempts to tamper with equipment to avoid screening are flagged.
Prioritizing among these safeguards will require careful consideration of the advantages,
limitations, and costs of different approaches.

Conclusion
Benchtop synthesis devices are proliferating across a growing set of manufacturers, jurisdictions,
and synthesis methods, and their throughput now approaches scales previously only available
from mail-order synthesis providers. The full report and associated dataset will give
manufacturers, screening tool providers, policymakers, and biosecurity advocates a shared
understanding of the benchtop synthesis landscape, helping to inform the development of
effective technical safeguards and policy frameworks.
